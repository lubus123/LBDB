Mission briefing

Two weeks, five bases, a Hiroshima strike, two pilgrim walks and a night in the hot water

Tokyo for four nights, the Tokaido shinkansen to Kyoto for five with day trips to Hiroshima and Nara, then south into the Kii mountains for the Kumano Kodo: walk the last 10 km of the pilgrim road into Hongu Taisha and over the pass to the hot springs, sleep there, then walk the old road up to Nachi's pagoda and waterfall the next day. Two nights in Osaka, and a last night at Haneda for the 09:35 flight.

    Kumano Kodo logistics

    • Buses. Kumano Kotsu's Hongu line runs Kii-Tanabe ⇄ Hosshinmon-oji ⇄ Hongu Taisha ⇄ Yunomine ⇄ Kawayu about every 1 to 2 hours, 2 hours end to end, about ¥2,100. Not covered by any JR pass; pay with cash or IC card. Screenshot the timetable from tb-kumano.jp the week before, in particular the 10:10 from Tanabe on 6 Nov and the 08:00 to Shingu on 7 Nov.
    • Bags. Send the suitcases from the Kyoto hotel to the Osaka hotel by takkyubin on the morning of 6 Nov (about ¥2,500 each, arrives 7 Nov, the day you check in). Carry a daypack each for the one mountain night. Tell the Osaka hotel in the booking note.
    • Trail. Walk in on 6 Nov: the Nakahechi's last 6.9 km from Hosshinmon-oji down into Hongu Taisha, then the Dainichi-goe 3.4 km over the ridge to Yunomine. Walk out on 7 Nov: the old road from Nachi station to Daimon-zaka, 3.5 km, then the cobbled stairs to Nachi Taisha and the falls. Both waymarked; no navigation skill needed. Water at Hosshinmon-oji and Hongu only on the first; carry 2 litres.
    • Hongu side. Tsuboyu at Yunomine is the only UNESCO-listed hot spring you can bathe in: a two-person rock bath, 30-minute slots, ¥800, first come. The 90 °C yutsubo next to it cooks eggs and sweet potatoes bought at the shop.
    • Nachi. From Hongu there is no direct bus to Nachi; it is bus to Shingu (1h20 from Yunomine, ¥1,540), then JR to Nachi station, then on foot up the old road, or bus to Daimon-zaka (20 min) if the legs say so. Kumano Hayatama Taisha is a 15-minute walk from Shingu station and makes the three grand shrines.

    Getting around

    • IC card. Non-Japanese Android phones cannot run Mobile Suica. Buy a physical Welcome Suica at the arrival airport (28-day validity, no deposit). Works on every metro, JR local line, bus and konbini nationwide, Kumano buses included.
    • Narita → hotel. Narita Express to Tokyo Station, ¥3,070, 60 min, then taxi or Marunouchi line to Akasaka.
    • Kyoto. Buses are slow in November; use the Karasuma and Tozai subway lines, JR, and Keihan where possible. Fushimi Inari is two JR stops from Kyoto Station.
    • Connectivity. eSIM bought before departure; offline Google Maps for Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, Hiroshima and the whole Kii peninsula. Signal is patchy on the trail.
    Intel

    Rules of engagement

    • Sat 31 Oct, Halloween. Shibuya station area is barricaded, street drinking banned from 18:00. Shibuya is on 29 Oct instead; Shinjuku on the 31st.
    • Tue 3 Nov, Culture Day. National holiday. Fushimi Inari at 07:00 before the crowds, Arashiyama after, Kinkaku-ji late afternoon.
    • Daylight. Sunrise about 06:20, sunset about 16:55 in early November. The walk in reaches Yunomine about 16:30; the walk out is done by 15:00. Headtorch in the pack regardless.
    • Weather. Kii peninsula is the wettest part of Honshu. Rain shell, not an umbrella, on the trail. Typhoon season is over by November.
    • Ryokan timing. Check in by 17:00, dinner is served at the stated time and cannot be moved. On 6 Nov the 10:10 bus and the turn-back rule on the day card protect that; tell the inn you arrive on foot about 16:30.
    • Onsen etiquette. Wash before entering, no swimwear, hair up, small towel stays out of the water. Tattoos: ask at check-in or book a private bath; Tsuboyu is private by design.
    • Hiroshima. Miyajima first, before 09:30, to beat the tour groups on the ferry. Peace Memorial Museum is open to 18:00, last entry 17:30, ¥200, no reservation. Miyajima charges a ¥100 visitor tax.
    • Haneda exfil. Flight 09:35 means at Terminal 3 by 07:30. From Tenkubashi it is one Keikyu stop; from the Kamata hotels it is 10 min.
    • Cash. Cards work in cities. Kumano buses, minshuku, Tsuboyu, shrines and Miyajima stalls want yen. Withdraw at 7-Eleven in Kyoto before 6 Nov; there is one ATM in Hongu and none on the trail.
    • Visa paperwork. The visa itinerary was filed for 31 Oct to 13 Nov. A shift inside the visa's validity window is fine if it is already issued; if still pending, resubmit with the new dates.

    Alternatives held in reserve

    • Hakone instead of Kumano. If the Kii trip gets cut: Osaka → Odawara Hikari, ryokan Fukuzumiro in Tonosawa (€399 with kaiseki, 9.4) or Hakone Miyanoshita Myojokan (€157, 9.2), Hakone loop next morning, Kodama to Shinagawa.
    • The long march instead of the loop. Sleep in Tanabe on the 5th (Comfort Hotel Kii Tanabe, €110, 9.1), take the 06:35 bus to Takijiri and walk the classic 17 km Takijiri → Takahara → Chikatsuyu → Tsugizakura, bus to Hongu, one onsen night instead of two. More trail, less bath, an early alarm.
    • Finish at the waterfall on foot. From Hongu, the Kogumotori-goe (13 km) to Koguchi and the Ogumotori-goe (14.5 km, 800 m climb) to Nachi Taisha the next day. Two hard days, beds in Koguchi booked through Kumano Travel, 06:30 starts. The pilgrim's ending; kept in reserve.
    • Two onsen nights after all. Sleep in Kii-Katsuura on 7 Nov at Hotel Urashima (€180, 8.3), the Bokido cave onsen opening onto the Pacific and a tuna breakfast; Osaka drops to one night and the 8th is a long haul.
    • One big loop instead of two walks. Two nights at Hongu, the 16.5 km Akagi-goe, Nakahechi and Dainichi-goe loop from the inn's door on the middle day, Nachi by bus on the way out. More bath, one fewer Kyoto night.

    Standard operating procedures

    Quick reference handbook

    Culture, language, customs, rations

    Read once on the plane, then look things up at the gate.

    How Japan works

    The operating principle

    • Wa, harmony: don't make a scene, don't make others uncomfortable. Volume down, patience up.
    • Omotenashi, hospitality: service is impeccable and tipping is an insult. Say thank you, bow a little, that is the payment.
    • Rules are followed because everyone follows them, not because someone is watching. Red man, you wait, even at 02:00 on an empty road.
    • "Chotto..." ("a little...") with a head tilt means no. Nobody will say no outright.
    • Punctuality is absolute. A train that leaves at 09:02 leaves at 09:02.

    Bowing and greeting

    • A nod of the head is enough from a visitor. Deeper bows are for apologies and formal thanks.
    • No handshakes unless offered. No hugging.
    • Shop staff shout "irasshaimase" (welcome) at you. Do not reply; it is not a question.
    • Receive anything handed to you with both hands, especially a business card or change on the tray.

    Public behaviour

    • No phone calls on trains or buses. Texting is fine. Phones on silent ("manner mode").
    • No eating while walking in cities. Festival stalls and Miyajima's street are the exception.
    • No smoking on the street outside marked areas; fines apply.
    • Queue at the floor markings. Let people off before you board.
    • Carry your rubbish. Bins are at konbini and stations only.
    • Blowing your nose in public is rude; sniffing is fine. Masks if you have a cold.
    • Escalators: stand left in Tokyo and Kyoto, right in Osaka.

    Shoes, floors, baths

    • Step up means shoes off: ryokan, temples, some restaurants, fitting rooms. Slippers are provided; toilet slippers are separate and stay in the toilet.
    • Never step on tatami in slippers or shoes. Socks or bare feet.
    • Yukata: left side over right. Right over left is how the dead are dressed.
    • Onsen: wash seated at the taps first, no swimwear, hair tied up, small towel on your head. No photos, ever.

    Eating and drinking

    At the table

    • Say "itadakimasu" before eating and "gochisousama deshita" when you leave.
    • Chopsticks: never stand them upright in rice, never pass food chopstick to chopstick (both are funeral rites). Use the rest or the wrapper. Do not point with them.
    • Slurping noodles is correct. Lifting the bowl to your mouth is correct.
    • Soy sauce goes on the fish side of nigiri, not the rice. Wasabi is already in there.
    • The wet towel (oshibori) is for hands, not face.
    • Pour for others, let them pour for you. "Kanpai" and wait for everyone before drinking.
    • Izakaya add a seating charge (otoshi) of ¥300 to ¥500 per head with a small dish. Normal.

    Ordering and paying

    • Ramen shops often use a ticket machine by the door: pay, take the ticket, hand it over.
    • Call staff with "sumimasen"; there is no hovering. A button on the table does the same job.
    • Pay at the register by the door, not at the table. Put cash or card on the tray.
    • No tipping, no splitting bills in small places; one person pays and you settle after.
    • Lunch sets (teishoku) at 11:30 are half the dinner price for the same kitchen.
    • Plastic food in the window is the menu. Point.
    • Konbini food is good, not a compromise. Egg sandwiches, onigiri, fried chicken, hot coffee.

    Shrines, temples, sacred ground

    Shrine (Shinto, torii gate)

    • Bow once at the torii. Walk at the side of the path; the middle is for the kami.
    • At the water basin: ladle in right hand, rinse left, swap, rinse right, pour into the left hand to rinse the mouth, never the ladle.
    • At the hall: coin in the box (¥5 is lucky), ring the bell, bow twice, clap twice, pray, bow once.
    • Kumano's three grand shrines (Hongu, Hayatama, Nachi) are the point of the pilgrimage; collect the stamps (goshuin, ¥300 to ¥500) in a book bought at Hongu.

    Temple (Buddhist)

    • Incense: light, wave out (never blow), place in the burner, waft the smoke over yourself.
    • Coin, bow, hands together, no clapping.
    • Hats off indoors. Photos usually fine outside, usually banned of the main image inside; look for the sign.
    • Most close at 17:00. Gardens are the reason to go early.

    Trains

    Shinkansen

    • Reserved cars are numbered; find your car marking on the platform floor. Non-reserved cars are usually 1 to 3.
    • Bags over 160 cm total need the oversized-baggage seat, free to book in SmartEX. Daypacks go on the rack.
    • Eating is fine on the shinkansen; buy an ekiben and a beer on the concourse.
    • Trains stop for about 60 seconds. Be at the door with your bags before the station.

    Local trains and metro

    • Tap the IC card in and out. Minimum balance to enter the gate is about ¥150; top up at any machine with cash.
    • Priority seats are for the elderly, pregnant, injured. Stand if in doubt.
    • Women-only cars run in rush hours on some lines, marked pink on the platform.
    • Google Maps tells you the platform, the car to board and the exit. Trust it.
    • Last trains are around midnight. Taxis after that are expensive but honest.

    Tips from the field

    • • Carry a small hand towel. Public toilets often have no dryers.
    • • Temples close around 17:00. Big sights in the morning, markets and neighbourhoods late afternoon.
    • • A coin purse earns its keep: ¥1, ¥5, ¥10, ¥50, ¥100, ¥500 pile up fast.
    • • Pharmacies sell most things. Bring prescription meds with the prescription; pseudoephedrine products are banned.
    • • Kyoto Station lockers fill by 10:00; use the staffed luggage counter instead.
    • • Department store basements (depachika) are the best cheap lunch in any city; everything is discounted after 19:00.
    • • On the trail, greet other walkers with "konnichiwa". Locals in the villages will say it first.
    • • Vending machines sell hot coffee and hot tea in November; the red-labelled ones are warm.
